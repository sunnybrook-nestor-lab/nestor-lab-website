# Research

Our research spans four interconnected platforms, united by the goal of advancing precision approaches to psychiatric treatment through neuromodulation, metabolic interventions, brain mapping, and psychedelic therapies.

## Metabolic-Neuromodulation Platform

The Metabolic-Neuromodulation Platform investigates how metabolic interventions can enhance the effects of brain stimulation therapies for treatment-resistant depression.

### ALIGN: Can a ketogenic diet boost brain stimulation for depression?

Up to a third of people with depression don't respond to standard antidepressants. A growing body of research suggests the brain's energy metabolism plays a role in mood — and that a ketogenic diet, which shifts the body toward burning fat for fuel, may influence the brain circuits involved in depression.

ALIGN tests whether pairing a ketogenic diet with brain stimulation works better than stimulation with a standard healthy diet. Sixty adults with treatment-resistant depression are randomly assigned to follow either a ketogenic diet or a Canadian Food Guide–aligned diet. Both groups receive the same treatment: a personalized, MRI-guided course of accelerated intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS), a fast form of non-invasive brain stimulation. The diet begins three weeks before stimulation and continues for twelve weeks.

Alongside depression symptoms, the study tracks ketone levels, blood-based metabolic markers, and brain connectivity on MRI — and asks a practical question: can people realistically follow a ketogenic diet during an intensive treatment course? ALIGN is led by the Harquail Centre for Neuromodulation at Sunnybrook, with support from the Baszucki Group.

**Key studies:**
- **ALIGN Trial** — Adjunctive ketogenic diet combined with intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) for treatment-resistant depression
- Metabolic biomarkers of neuromodulation treatment response
- qEEG correlates of iTBS response

## Causal Brain Mapping Studies

Using interleaved TMS-fMRI and MRS, this platform maps the causal circuits underlying neuromodulation therapies, providing mechanistic insight into how brain stimulation works.

### TMS-fMRI: Watching brain stimulation work in real time

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is an effective depression treatment, but roughly a third of people don't respond — and we can't yet predict who will. Part of the problem is that we don't fully understand what rTMS does to the brain's mood circuits as it happens.

This study uses a technically demanding approach called interleaved TMS-fMRI: delivering magnetic stimulation pulses while a person is inside an MRI scanner, so we can watch the targeted mood circuit — connecting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to deeper emotion-regulating regions — respond in real time. We focus on intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS), a fast, patterned form of rTMS, in people whose depression has resisted multiple treatments.

By mapping how the circuit engages during stimulation, the study aims to explain why some people respond and others don't, help personalize rTMS, and separate genuine treatment effects from placebo. It is led by the Harquail Centre for Neuromodulation at Sunnybrook.

## Advanced Neuromodulation Trials

This platform houses clinical trials applying novel neuromodulation protocols to specific populations, including late-life depression (LLD) and post-concussion syndrome (PCS).

### ACT-LLD: Faster brain stimulation for depression in older adults

Depression is common in later life and often doesn't respond to medication. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) — using focused magnetic pulses to gently stimulate mood-related brain regions — is an effective, well-tolerated alternative, but a standard course means travelling to a clinic every weekday for about six weeks. That's a real barrier for many older adults.

ACT-LLD asks a simple question: can we deliver the same treatment far more quickly? The trial compares an **accelerated** schedule — several short sessions a day over about six days — against the **conventional** once-daily, six-week course, in adults aged 60 and older with hard-to-treat depression. If the accelerated approach works as well, it could dramatically cut the time and travel involved and open treatment to more people.

ACT-LLD is a randomized trial led by the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH), conducted with the Harquail Centre for Neuromodulation at Sunnybrook and funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR).

### PCS: Brain stimulation for persistent concussion symptoms

Most people recover from a concussion within weeks, but some are left with lasting problems — trouble concentrating, low mood, fatigue, and headaches — known as post-concussion syndrome. Treatment options are limited.

This open-label pilot study tests whether accelerated intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) — a quick, non-invasive form of brain stimulation that uses magnetic pulses — can ease those persistent symptoms. Each participant's stimulation is guided by their own MRI scan, so the pulses are aimed precisely at the brain regions involved. We also use functional MRI before and after treatment to see how the post-concussion brain responds.

Because this is an early-stage pilot, everyone receives active treatment, and the focus is on safety, feasibility, and the changes in brain function and symptoms that follow. The study is led by Dr. Sean Nestor and Dr. Matthew Burke at Sunnybrook's Harquail Centre for Neuromodulation.

## Psychedelics Platform (EMBRACE)

The EMBRACE study explores psilocybin-assisted therapy for depression, investigating psychedelic interventions within a clinical neuroscience framework.

### EMBRACE: How does psilocybin change the depressed brain?

About a third of people with depression don't fully respond to standard treatments like antidepressants or therapy. Psilocybin — the active compound in "magic mushrooms" — has shown promise as a rapid-acting antidepressant in early trials, but exactly how it works in the brain isn't yet understood.

EMBRACE is a randomized, placebo-controlled neuroimaging study of 50 adults with depression. Participants receive either a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin or an inactive placebo in a supportive clinical setting; three weeks later, those who received placebo are given psilocybin as well. Using advanced MRI — measuring both blood flow and brain activity — we track how psilocybin reshapes the brain networks involved in mood, both within hours of a dose and over the weeks that follow.

The goal is to separate psilocybin's biological effects from the placebo response, and to learn whether early brain changes can predict who benefits. EMBRACE is led by the Harquail Centre for Neuromodulation at Sunnybrook, with funding from the Sunnybrook Foundation and CIHR.
